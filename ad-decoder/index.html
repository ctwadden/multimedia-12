Beginning Graphic Design: Layout & Composition

Video unavailable? Read this instead.

A layout is the arrangement of images and words. Designers can make some elements larger, use stronger contrast, or leave more space around them. These choices can help a viewer notice important information.

Related items can be grouped together. Alignment gives elements a shared edge or line. Repeating colours or lettering styles can connect parts of a design.

Today, focus on the order of attention: first, next, last. Then consider how the image and its visual style support the message.

9–17 minutes · Concept 01

Visual hierarchy: first, next, last.

Visual hierarchy is the order of importance a design communicates. Size, contrast, lettering and spacing can make one element more noticeable than another.

There is no rule that the product must always come first. The order should support the particular advertisement’s purpose.

ContrastA noticeable difference, such as large/small, light/dark or thick/thin.
Negative spaceThe space around and between elements. It does not have to be white.

Client brief: “People should notice our headphones first, then read the headline.”

Same product illustration, words and palette. Different sizes, spacing and arrangement. Both are fictional teaching advertisements.

1. What catches your eye?

Look at headphone versions A and B. What do you notice first in each? Identify the visible design choice that causes the difference.

“In A, I notice … first. In B, I notice … first. The difference is caused by …”

Write your response under Question 1 in your Google Doc.

2. Which version fits the brief?

The client wants the headphones noticed first. Choose A or B and explain how one choice involving size, contrast or negative space supports that goal.

“Version … better supports this brief because … This directs attention to …”

Write your response under Question 2 in your Google Doc.

Go beyond preference: “I like it” is not enough. Name a visible feature and explain what it does. Your first impression may differ from a classmate’s.

4
17–25 minutes · Concept 02

Visual metaphor: show one thing, suggest another.

A visual metaphor uses an image to connect one thing with another idea. A battery shown as a marathon runner, for example, could suggest endurance. It would not prove how long the battery lasts.

Literal: what you can actually see. Suggested meaning: what you think that combination communicates. Explain the connection rather than guessing a hidden “correct” answer.

Look first without a headline. This fictional advertisement is for a sketchbook. Study how the product and the unexpected object connect.

3. Decode the connection

What does the sketchbook image literally show? What might it suggest about drawing or creativity? Explain the connection using a visible detail.

“The image literally shows … It might suggest … because …”

Answer Question 3 in your Doc before trying the headlines.

Now try a headline

Click each option to place it beneath the same image. Compare the connection between the words and the picture.

4. Make the words and image work together

Which headline makes the clearest connection with this image: A, “Open your imagination,” or B, “Keep everything organised”? Explain how the words and a visible feature support one another.

“Headline … fits because the word(s) … connect with … in the image.”

Write your response under Question 4 in your Google Doc.

Image + words: an unusual image alone is not automatically an effective ad. Its connection to the product and message still needs to make sense.

5
25–34 minutes · Concept 03

Visual tone: the same product, a different impression.

In this lesson, visual tone means the overall impression a design creates. Colour choices and typography—the style and arrangement of lettering—can contribute to that impression.

Consider the client’s purpose and the viewer’s needs. Colours and fonts do not have one fixed meaning for everyone. Write “could suggest” or “to me,” then point to evidence.

Aa · Bold
Lettering cluesThick/thin strokes, rounded/sharp shapes, close/wide spacing. Serif letters have small finishing strokes; sans-serif letters do not.
Aa · Serif
Colour cluesVivid/muted, warm/cool, light/dark, high/low contrast. Name a colour and explain its effect in this design.

Client brief: “Make this lamp feel right for a quiet, comfortable reading corner.”

The lamp illustration, wording, positions and sizes are unchanged. The palette and lettering style change. These are fictional teaching advertisements, not claims about an actual product.

5. Match the visual tone to the brief

Which lamp version better fits the quiet reading-corner brief? Identify one colour choice and one lettering feature, and explain how each supports your interpretation.

“I would choose version … The colour … could suggest … The lettering … supports this because …”

Write your response under Question 5 in your Google Doc.

The brief changes: “Now make the same lamp feel right for a lively, creative workspace.”

6. Change the direction—without opening Photoshop

Choose a lamp version to adapt. Recommend one specific colour change and one lettering change for the new brief. Explain how each change could help. Keep the words and lamp the same.

“Starting with version …, I would change … to … because … I would change the lettering to … because …”

Describe your changes under Question 6 in your Doc. Do not make a new advertisement.

Keep it readable. Match a situation and a need, not a stereotype about an age or gender.

Finished all six? Optional stretch question.

Choose one example. How might someone interpret it differently from you because of a different experience or context? Refer to a visible detail. Add 1–2 sentences in the optional space in your Doc.
