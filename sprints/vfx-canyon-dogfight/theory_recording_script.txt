SLIDE 01 — What you are going to make  (save your recording as audio/theory/slide-01.mp3)
Here is the shot you are going to build. A fighter chases an X-wing through a desert canyon. A second X-wing comes the other way and shoots the fighter down. It is eight seconds long. It looks complicated, but it is made of simple parts, and you will build them one at a time. First the flying. Then the lasers. Then the explosion. Then the polish. This deck explains the ideas. The workbook shows every click.

SLIDE 02 — Two programs, two jobs  (save your recording as audio/theory/slide-02.mp3)
Studios split this work in two. A 3D program makes the things that need depth: ships, a canyon, a moving camera. A compositing program then combines pictures. Why not do everything in 3D? Because 3D is slow to change. If your glow is too strong, re-rendering takes minutes. In the compositor you drag one slider and see it straight away. The bridge between the two programs is called render passes. You save the same shot several times, each showing one thing: the normal picture, how far away everything is, and only the things that glow.

SLIDE 03 — Flying on rails  (save your recording as audio/theory/slide-03.mp3)
You will not animate the ships by hand, frame by frame. You draw one curve down the middle of the canyon. Then you tell each ship to follow that curve. That instruction is called a constraint. The constraint has one number, called Offset Factor. Zero means the start of the curve, one means the end. Set it low at frame one, higher at the last frame, and the ship flies the whole canyon. Two keyframes, not two hundred.

SLIDE 04 — A chase is the same path, a little behind  (save your recording as audio/theory/slide-04.mp3)
A chase is three things on the same path, each a little behind the one in front. The X-wing leads. The enemy fighter follows forty-three metres back. The camera rides thirty metres behind that. Because they share the curve, every bend you draw is flown by all three, one after another. Change the curve once and the whole chase changes.

SLIDE 05 — What makes it feel fast  (save your recording as audio/theory/slide-05.mp3)
Speed on screen does not come from a big number. It comes from things passing close to the lens. That is why we fly low between canyon walls and past rock spires, and not high above clouds. Three more tricks. The ships bank, they lean into each bend. The camera does not stare at the ship; it looks at a point a little ahead on the path, so the ship slides around the frame. And motion blur smears everything that moves fast.

SLIDE 06 — A laser bolt is just a glowing stick that moves  (save your recording as audio/theory/slide-06.mp3)
A laser bolt is a short glowing stick that moves in a straight line. You could key each one by hand, but an add-on called PewPew does it for you. You put an emitter on the gun and point it forward. You go to the frame where you want a shot, and press the button marked Pew. The add-on builds the bolt, a muzzle flash, and, if the bolt hits something, sparks and a scorch mark. Bolts take time to travel. Ours move seventeen and a half metres every frame. A target a hundred metres away is hit about six frames after you fire.

SLIDE 07 — Render three pictures, not one  (save your recording as audio/theory/slide-07.mp3)
Before leaving Blender you render the shot three times. The first is the normal picture. We call it the beauty pass. The second is the mist pass. It is a grey picture where near things are black and far things are white. The compositor uses it to add haze only in the distance. The third is a trick: turn off the sun and the sky and render again. Now the only things you can see are things that make their own light. Lasers, engines, flashes. That picture lets you add glow to exactly those things and nothing else.

SLIDE 08 — Layers and blend modes  (save your recording as audio/theory/slide-08.mp3)
In After Effects, everything is a layer, and layers stack from the bottom up. Each layer has a blend mode, which says how it mixes with what is underneath. Normal just covers. Add is for light: it adds brightness, so black areas vanish and bright areas glow. Use Add for glows, sparks and flares. Screen is a gentler version. One more idea: a track matte. You can make one layer control where another layer is visible. We use the mist picture as a matte for a pale haze colour, so the haze appears only where the canyon is far away.

SLIDE 09 — Real fire beats fake fire  (save your recording as audio/theory/slide-09.mp3)
Simulating fire in 3D is slow and hard to make look right. Filmed fire always looks right, because it is real. So visual effects artists keep libraries of explosions, sparks and smoke filmed against black. Your job is to make the clip belong to the shot. Put it where the fighter is. Scale it so it is the right size for that distance. Start it on the frame of the hit. Then keyframe its position so it stays on the wreck as the camera flies past.

SLIDE 10 — The camera layer: flare, grade, grain, bars  (save your recording as audio/theory/slide-10.mp3)
The last layers pretend there was a real camera. A real lens flares when it points near the sun. Real film has grain, and lenses are darker at the corners. A colour grade adds contrast and pushes the colours. Black bars give the wide cinema shape. One rule for all of these. If the audience notices the effect, it is too strong. Turn it up until you can see it, then turn it back down a little.

SLIDE 11 — Before you open Blender  (save your recording as audio/theory/slide-11.mp3)
You are ready to start. You will build the demo exactly first, so you learn the method. At the end, a director’s card will ask you to change the shot, and that change is what you are assessed on. Before you open Blender, pause and answer three questions from memory. Which three things share the flight path? What does each render pass show? And name one blend mode and what it is for. Then open the workbook.
