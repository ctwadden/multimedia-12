SLIDE 01 — A walk is a personality  (save your recording as audio/theory/slide-01.mp3)
You can recognise a friend from the far end of a corridor, just from the way they walk. A walk tells you who someone is and how they feel. In these lessons you take one character and one pair of legs, and you learn how to make that character walk like four different people.

SLIDE 02 — Contact, down, passing, up  (save your recording as audio/theory/slide-02.mp3)
Every walk is built from four key poses for each step. Contact, where the heel touches down and the legs are at their widest. Down, where the weight lands and the body is at its lowest. Passing, where one leg passes the other. And up, where the back foot pushes off and the body is at its highest.

SLIDE 03 — Six things you can turn  (save your recording as audio/theory/slide-03.mp3)
There are six things you can change. Think of them as dials. Stride is how far apart the feet land. Lift is how high they come off the ground. Then the hips, how high they ride and how much they bob, and the lean of the body. Then the arms. And last, the number of frames. Most personalities only need two or three dials turned a long way.

SLIDE 04 — Same drawings, different speed  (save your recording as audio/theory/slide-04.mp3)
Timing is the dial the audience feels first. The normal walk takes twenty four frames for two steps. The tired walk takes thirty six, the sneak takes forty, and the happy walk only twenty. You change the timing in the Dope Sheet, by selecting all the keyframes and scaling them.

SLIDE 05 — Before you draw  (save your recording as audio/theory/slide-05.mp3)
You will plan a walk, draw the key poses, set the timing, and then take a note from the director. Before you draw, answer three questions from memory. Name the four key poses in order. Name three of the dials. And which is faster, a twenty frame cycle or a thirty six frame cycle? Then open the workbook.
