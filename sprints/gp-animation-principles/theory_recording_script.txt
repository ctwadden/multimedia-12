SLIDE 01 — Moving is not the same as alive  (save your recording as audio/theory/slide-01.mp3)
You can already make things move in Grease Pencil. This set of lessons is about making them feel alive. There are four ideas. Spacing shows weight. Anticipation shows that a character has decided to do something. And arcs and follow through show what a thing is made of.

SLIDE 02 — Close is slow. Far apart is fast.  (save your recording as audio/theory/slide-02.mp3)
Timing is how many drawings an action takes. Spacing is where each of those drawings sits. When drawings are close together, the move is slow. When they are far apart, it is fast. Heavy things take time to get going and time to stop, so you bunch the drawings at the start and at the end. That is called slow out and slow in.

SLIDE 03 — Ready, go, land  (save your recording as audio/theory/slide-03.mp3)
Every action has three parts. The preparation, the action, and the reaction. The preparation is called anticipation, and it goes the opposite way to the action. Before you jump up, you crouch down. It tells the audience what is about to happen, and it makes the action look powerful. The bigger the wind up, the bigger the action reads.

SLIDE 04 — Joints swing, so paths curve  (save your recording as audio/theory/slide-04.mp3)
Living things are built from joints, and joints swing. An arm is a fixed length, swinging from the shoulder, so the hand travels on a curve, called an arc. If you put the in between drawing exactly halfway between two positions on the page, the arm gets shorter in the middle. An in between is where the joint would really be.

SLIDE 05 — Loose parts arrive late  (save your recording as audio/theory/slide-05.mp3)
When a body moves, its loose parts drag behind. When the body stops, they keep going, and swing past where they will end up. That is the overshoot. Then they settle, in smaller and smaller swings. This is called follow through, and it is why a good stop feels soft and not like hitting a wall.

SLIDE 06 — Before you draw  (save your recording as audio/theory/slide-06.mp3)
Four exercises, then one shot of your own. Before you draw, answer three questions from memory. Which one means where the drawings sit: timing or spacing? Which way does an anticipation go? And what does a tail do when the body stops? Then open the workbook.
